Practical Guide

Deposition prep checklist for Louisiana attorneys.

What to confirm with your court reporter before the day of testimony — so nothing goes wrong on the record.

Most deposition-day problems trace back to something the noticing attorney could have closed out a week earlier with one email or one phone call. This checklist covers the things experienced litigation paralegals and trial lawyers confirm in advance, organized roughly in the order they come up.

Two weeks out

  • Confirm reporter availability and CCR status. For Louisiana state-court matters, the reporter should be a Certified Court Reporter authorized to administer oaths. Get this in writing on the confirmation.
  • Confirm location and parking. If the deposition is at a conference room you don't usually use, request the reporting agency's standard logistics sheet — building access, parking validation, security check-in.
  • Decide on real-time delivery. If you want real-time on attorney laptops or iPads, the agency needs to know in advance so they can dispatch a reporter with the right CART software setup. Walking in and asking for real-time the morning of typically isn't possible.
  • Decide on video. If you want a video deposition, confirm that the same agency is booking and coordinating both the reporter and the videographer — not that you have to hire the videographer separately. Single-agency coordination produces tighter video/transcript sync and one invoice instead of two.
  • Confirm the deposition platform for remote participants. Zoom and Teams are most common in Louisiana. Test the link with any remote attorney and the witness ahead of time, particularly if the witness is in a rural parish with marginal bandwidth.
  • Confirm interpreter, if needed. For non-English witnesses, the reporting agency typically books a certified interpreter; lead time of 7–10 days is normal. Spanish, Vietnamese, and French are commonly requested in Louisiana.

One week out

  • Send the notice and witness contact info. The reporter and videographer call the witness ahead for remote setups; they need the witness's email and phone.
  • Send the case caption and counsel list. The reporter uses these on the title page, on appearance lines, and on the certificate. Last-minute changes the morning of are doable but rough.
  • Decide on exhibit handling. For remote depositions, the reporting agency's exhibit platform (or a shared link) matters more than the underlying video platform. Confirm how exhibits will be presented to the witness — uploaded ahead, shared during, or both.
  • Discuss expedites. If you need a rough draft or expedited final, confirm pricing and turnaround in writing now. Asking for expedite at the end of the deposition is the most expensive way to do it.
  • Confirm read & sign. Whether the witness reads and signs is usually negotiated on the record, but if you know your position, tell the reporting agency so the certificate is set up correctly.

The day before

  • Confirm the reporter and videographer by name. A short email confirming the assigned reporter is en route reduces the chance of a no-show.
  • Verify exhibit numbering. If multiple parties are introducing exhibits, agree in advance whether numbering is global (1, 2, 3...) or per-party (Plaintiff-1, Defendant-1...). Disagreements about exhibit numbers on the record waste real time.
  • For remote depositions, test the platform. Have the witness join the platform briefly the day before. The number of depositions delayed an hour because the witness can't get audio working on the laptop is not small.
  • Confirm video reflection prep. For video depositions, the videographer typically asks for the witness to wear non-patterned clothing and to position camera/lights to avoid glare. The agency sends a short sheet for this.

The morning of

  • Have the courtesy copy of exhibits ready. Reporters appreciate a hard or digital copy of intended exhibits so they can match them on the record without delay.
  • Confirm the witness has water and a place to step away. Long depositions go more smoothly when the witness knows breaks are normal.
  • Stipulations on the record. The standard Louisiana stipulations — waiver of certain procedural formalities, objections reserved except form, read-and-sign procedure — go on the record at the start. Have the language ready.
  • For remote, designate a tech contact. If the witness drops off the call, who reconnects them? Decide ahead of time.

After the deposition

  • Confirm the transcript order. Most reporting agencies treat the noticing attorney as the orderer of the original; the cross-noticing party orders copies. Confirm what you're ordering before leaving.
  • Confirm video deliverable format. MP4 and synchronized PTX (or equivalent) are most common. Ask whether you want the synced version for trial or just the raw video.
  • Set expectations on read-and-sign timing. The 30-day clock under La. C.C.P. Art. 1445 starts when the certified transcript is delivered. If your witness lives far from your office, plan distribution accordingly.
  • Save the certificate page. The CCR certificate is your evidence of regularity. Don't lose it.
One general rule: the best reporting agencies are the ones where one person — a coordinator or scheduler — handles every detail above without bouncing you between departments. If you find yourself emailing three different people at the same agency for one deposition, it's worth shopping the next matter elsewhere.

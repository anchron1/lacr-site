What does "CCR-certified" mean in Louisiana?

Louisiana licenses court reporters through the Louisiana Board of Examiners of Certified Shorthand Reporters. A Certified Court Reporter (CCR) has passed both a written examination on legal procedure and a skills test demonstrating verbatim transcription at minimum speeds: 225 words per minute on testimony, 200 on jury charges, and 180 on literary material — all with at least 95% accuracy.

For depositions used in Louisiana state court proceedings, La. C.C.P. Art. 1434 generally requires that the reporter taking the testimony be authorized to administer oaths. CCRs are commissioned notaries under state law, which is why most Louisiana firms specifically request a CCR rather than a non-certified reporter or a remote-only transcription service.

Why this matters in practice: if you hire a reporter who is not authorized to administer oaths in Louisiana, the deposition transcript can be challenged on procedural grounds. A CCR designation eliminates that risk by default.

Choosing a court reporter in Louisiana

Most attorneys who run a lot of depositions develop a short list of two or three reporting agencies they trust and rotate through them. If you're building that list — or your usual reporter is unavailable — these are the criteria that matter:

1. Local coverage in the parish where the deposition is taking place

Louisiana is geographically broad. A New Orleans-based agency without reporters who live and work near Shreveport will quote you a higher rate (to cover travel) and may not have backup if the assigned reporter falls ill. Agencies with statewide reach through actual local relationships — not just contractors flown in — produce better outcomes for out-of-parish depositions.

2. Coordinated reporter and video under one agency

The agencies attorneys complain about most are the ones where you have to separately book and coordinate the videographer. Coordination breaks down, video and transcript sync fails, and someone ends up making frantic phone calls the morning of. Agencies that book and manage both the reporter and the videographer for you — under one engagement and one invoice — are worth a premium because the coordination burden stays with them, not you.

3. Turnaround and expedite policy

Standard transcript turnaround in Louisiana is 10–14 business days. Expedited turnaround (24–72 hour) is common but pricing varies wildly. Ask in advance what expedite costs and whether the agency can produce a rough draft via real-time for use at the next deposition the same week.

4. Remote deposition platform

Since 2020, the majority of Louisiana depositions involve at least one remote participant. The platform should be neutral, secure (encrypted in transit), produce an exhibit-handling workflow that doesn't lose evidence in chat, and not require the witness to install software the morning of testimony. Zoom, Teams, and proprietary platforms are all common; what matters is that the agency has tested theirs across the bandwidth of rural Louisiana.

5. A single point of contact

Multi-day matters with multiple deponents in multiple cities live or die on scheduling discipline. The agencies attorneys rebook are the ones where a single coordinator handles every detail — reporter, videographer, interpreter, conference room, exhibits — rather than passing you between departments.
